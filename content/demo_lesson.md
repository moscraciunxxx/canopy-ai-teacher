# Solving One-Step and Two-Step Linear Equations

## Lesson snapshot

**Grade band:** 7–8 mathematics  
**Focus:** Solving equations in one variable using inverse operations  
**Essential question:** How can we find the value that makes an equation true while keeping both sides balanced?

An equation is a statement that two expressions have the same value. The equals sign does not mean “the answer comes next”; it means “is the same as.” A variable, such as x, stands for an unknown number. Solving an equation means finding the value of the variable that makes the entire statement true. That value is the **solution**.

This mini-lesson uses a balance model and inverse operations. It is designed to be teachable in a short demonstration, but the examples and prompts also give students enough structure to explain their reasoning.

## Learning targets

By the end, you should be able to:

1. Explain why an equation must stay balanced when you change it.
2. Solve one-step equations involving addition, subtraction, multiplication, or division.
3. Solve two-step equations by undoing operations in the reverse order in which they act on the variable.
4. Check a solution by substituting it into the original equation and explain any error clearly.

## 1. The balance idea

Imagine an equation as a balanced scale. In x+4=11, the left side has an unknown amount plus 4, and the right side has 11. If we remove 4 from the left, we must remove 4 from the right as well. The balance remains level:

x+4=11  
x+4−4=11−4  
x=7

The move “subtract 4 from both sides” is not a trick. It is a legal change because subtracting the same amount from equal quantities keeps them equal. The same principle works for adding, multiplying, or dividing both sides by the same nonzero number.

The goal is to get the variable by itself. Each step should make the equation simpler without changing its solution.

It can help to distinguish an expression from an equation. An expression such as 2x+5 has no claim that it equals a particular number. An equation such as 2x+5=17 makes a claim that can be tested. The solution is not just the number produced by a sequence of steps; it is the number that makes the original claim true. This is why a check in the original equation is powerful: it tests the meaning of the answer, not merely whether the last line looks tidy.

## 2. One-step equations: use the inverse operation

An **inverse operation** undoes another operation. Addition and subtraction are inverses. Multiplication and division are inverses. Look at the operation attached to the variable, then use its inverse on both sides.

### Addition and subtraction

**Example 1: Solve x+8=23.**

The variable has 8 added to it. Subtract 8 from both sides:

x+8−8=23−8  
x=15

Check: 15+8=23, so the solution is x=15.

**Example 2: Solve y−6=14.**

The equation subtracts 6 from y. Add 6 to both sides:

y−6+6=14+6  
y=20

Check: 20−6=14. Notice the sign: adding 6 undoes subtracting 6.

### Multiplication and division

**Example 3: Solve 5a=40.**

The 5 is multiplying a. Divide both sides by 5:

5a ÷ 5=40 ÷ 5  
a=8

Check: 5(8)=40.

**Example 4: Solve b÷3=−4.**

The variable is being divided by 3. Multiply both sides by 3:

3(b÷3)=3(−4)  
b=−12

Check: −12÷3=−4. A negative solution is reasonable because a negative number divided by a positive number stays negative.

## 3. Two-step equations: undo the outside operation first

In a two-step equation, the variable is affected by two operations. For example, in 3x+5=20, x is first multiplied by 3 and then 5 is added. To reverse that process, undo the last operation first: subtract 5, then undo multiplication by dividing by 3.

### Worked example: 3x+5=20

**Step 1: Undo addition.** Subtract 5 from both sides.

3x+5−5=20−5  
3x=15

**Step 2: Undo multiplication.** Divide both sides by 3.

3x÷3=15÷3  
x=5

**Check in the original equation:** 3(5)+5=15+5=20. Therefore x=5.

The order matters. Dividing 20 by 3 before removing the 5 mixes two operations and generally does not isolate x. “Reverse order” is a shortcut for the balance reasoning: remove the constant term first, then remove the coefficient.

### When the coefficient is negative

**Worked example: −2m−7=9.**

Here, −7 is added to −2m. Add 7 to both sides:

−2m−7+7=9+7  
−2m=16

Now divide both sides by −2:

(−2m)÷(−2)=16÷(−2)  
m=−8

Check: −2(−8)−7=16−7=9. Dividing by a negative changes the sign of the result; it does not make the variable positive automatically.

### A variable divided by a number

**Worked example: n÷4+2=9.**

First undo the added 2:

n÷4=7

Then multiply by 4:

n=28

Check: 28÷4+2=7+2=9. The same inverse-operation idea works even when the coefficient is written as a division expression.

## 4. A dependable solve-and-check routine

Use this routine whenever you see a linear equation with one variable:

1. **Read the structure.** Identify the variable, its coefficient, and any constant being added or subtracted.
2. **Choose the inverse of the operation nearest the outside.** For a two-step equation, undo the constant term before the coefficient.
3. **Do the same thing to both sides.** Write the operation or show it in the next line so the balance is visible.
4. **Simplify one step at a time.** Combine only terms that can actually be combined; do not cancel across an equals sign.
5. **Check the original equation.** Substitute the proposed value for the variable. If both sides have the same value, the solution works.
6. **State the result.** Write x=..., and if a word problem is involved, include units and a sentence.

For 7p−4=31, the routine looks like this:

7p−4+4=31+4  
7p=35  
7p÷7=35÷7  
p=5

Check: 7(5)−4=35−4=31. The answer is p=5.

## 5. Common misconceptions and how to repair them

**Misconception 1: “Move the 5 across the equals sign and change its sign.”** Students may write 3x=20−5 without understanding why. The shortcut can produce the right line, but it becomes fragile with negatives, fractions, or more complex expressions. Repair it by saying: “Subtract 5 from both sides,” and show 3x+5−5=20−5. The canceled pair makes the balance rule visible.

**Misconception 2: Changing only one side.** A student might turn x+9=16 into x=16−9 and be unable to explain the step. Ask, “What happened to the left side, and what equal change happened on the right?” Have the student name the same operation on both sides, even when simplifying makes one side look shorter.

**Misconception 3: Reversing the sign incorrectly.** For x−7=2, some students subtract 7 again and get x=−5. Point to the operation actually shown: −7 is being added, so +7 is the inverse. Check the result; −5−7 is not 2.

**Misconception 4: Dividing the constant but not the whole side.** In 4x+8=24, a student may divide 24 by 4 while leaving 8 untouched. First subtract 8 to create 4x=16; then divide the entire equation by 4. A coefficient is attached to the variable term, not to a separate constant.

**Misconception 5: Treating 3x as 3+x.** The notation 3x means 3 times x. Ask the student to rewrite it as 3·x, then choose division by 3 as the inverse. This distinction also explains why 3(5)=15, not 8.

**Misconception 6: Stopping without checking.** A neat-looking x value can still be wrong. Substitution is a quick test, not busywork. If x=5 is proposed for 3x+5=20, evaluate 3(5)+5. The equal result confirms the solution and gives evidence for the explanation.

## 6. From equations to situations

Equations can represent real quantities. Suppose a music app charges a one-time fee of 6 dollars plus 4 dollars per month. After some months, the total is 26 dollars. Let m be the number of months:

4m+6=26  
4m=20 (subtract 6)  
m=5 (divide by 4)

The solution is 5 months. Check the context: 5 is a sensible whole-number count, and 4(5)+6=26. If an equation produces m=−2 months, the algebra might be valid but the situation would require interpretation; a negative time may not make sense in that context.

## 7. Transfer prompts

Try each prompt before reading the response target. The point is to transfer the routine, not to memorize one pattern.

1. **Explain:** In your own words, why must the same operation be performed on both sides of an equation?
2. **Solve:** q+13=−2.
3. **Solve and check:** 6r−5=31.
4. **Diagnose:** A student solves 2z+9=19 by writing 2z=19+9. What went wrong, and what should the next line be?
5. **Represent:** A taxi ride costs 3 dollars to start and 2 dollars per mile. The fare is 17 dollars. Write and solve an equation for the number of miles.
6. **Create:** Write a two-step equation whose solution is x=−4, then verify it by substitution.

Possible response targets: q=−15, r=6, and z=5. For the diagnosis, the student used the wrong inverse: subtract 9 from both sides to get 2z=10. The taxi equation is 2d+3=17, giving d=7 miles. Many equations can have solution −4; for example, 5x+6=−14 works because 5(−4)+6=−14.

## 8. Two-minute demonstration flow

For a quick demo, begin with the balance question: “If I subtract 5 from the left side, what must happen on the right?” Use 3x+5=20 and narrate the two inverse operations: subtract 5, then divide by 3. Ask the audience to predict the answer before the check. Next show the error 3x=20+5 and have them identify the incorrect inverse. Finish with the transfer prompt 6r−5=31; a strong response includes the operations on both sides, the solution r=6, and a substitution check. This sequence demonstrates concept, procedure, misconception detection, and independent transfer within two minutes.
